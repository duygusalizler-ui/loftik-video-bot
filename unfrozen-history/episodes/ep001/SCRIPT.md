## 4. Wattle and daub (POV)

## 5. A white roof

> Above us is thatch, as thick as my arm is long. Straw holds warm air the way wool does. Snow on top helps too; it seals the roof like a blanket. We never knock the snow off. My grandfather said a white roof is a warm house, and he was right.

## 6. The hearth (POV)

## 7. Smoke and bacon (POV)

> The smoke stings our eyes and makes us cough all winter. But it does good work too. It dries the thatch, it keeps the rot and the insects out of the rafters, and it cures the bacon hanging above us. In our house, nothing is wasted, not even smoke.

## 8. By hook or by crook (POV)

> The fire needs feeding every hour of every day. The lord owns the woods, but custom lets us take dead wood — whatever we can pull down with a hook or a shepherd's crook. We gather it all autumn: fallen branches, gorse, broom, bundles of brushwood we call faggots.

## 9. Counting the woodpile

> Look at that woodpile. Agnes counts it the way the lord's reeve counts sheep. If it shrinks too fast before Christmas, we burn less and sit closer. A big fire is a fool's fire. A small fire, kept alive all winter long, is the one that saves you.

## 10. The curfew (POV)

> At night we never let the fire die. We rake the embers into a heap, bury them under ash, and cover them with a clay lid we call the curfew. In the morning, a breath of air and a handful of straw wake them. Flint and steel is slow, cold work.

## 11. Our second house (POV)

> Our clothes are our second house. Next to the skin, a linen shirt. Over it, a woollen tunic, then another, then a thick hood over my shoulders. I wrap my legs in strips of wool, and stuff my shoes with hay. Wool keeps you warm even when it is wet.

## 12. Agnes spins

> Every thread we wear, Agnes spun herself. Even now, in the dark afternoons, her spindle never stops. The wool comes from our own four sheep. When the children outgrow a tunic, it is cut down, patched, and passed on. Last, it becomes rags that stuff the cracks in the wall.

## 13. Martinmas (POV)

> Our winter really begins at Martinmas, in November. That is when we kill the pig. There is no food for it in the cold months, and it would eat what we need. So it becomes bacon, sausages, lard and salt meat, packed in a barrel. Even the blood becomes pudding.

> The rest of our winter lives in sacks and pots. Dried peas and beans. Oats and barley. Onions and garlic hanging from the beams. A cheese or two, if the cow was kind. Apples wrapped in straw. Every sack must last until spring, and there is no market in the snow.

## 15. Pottage (POV)

> Every day we eat pottage. A pot hangs over the fire from morning to night, and whatever we have goes in: peas, oats, leeks, cabbage, a scrap of bacon for taste. The pot is rarely emptied; we just add to it. Hot food, eaten slowly, warms you from the inside.

> At the far end of the house, behind a wattle fence, lives our cow. Townsfolk laugh at that. Let them try a January night. A cow is a great warm beast that breathes like a bellows. She warms our house, and we keep her alive. It is a fair bargain.

> The hens roost in the rafters above her, and in winter they lay few eggs, so each one is a small feast. We feed the cow hay from the loft and pea straw. If the hay runs out before the grass returns, she starves, and so might we.

> Winter is no time for rest. Three days a week I owe the lord my labour, and now that means threshing his grain in the great barn with a flail, two sticks joined by leather. Thump, thump, thump, all day long. Hard work — but warm work, out of the wind.

## 20. Everyone works

> My own days are just as full. I mend the plough, cut hazel rods, and weave new hurdles for the sheep pens. Agnes brews and spins and bakes. Joan fetches water and minds the hens. Even little Hugh has his task: he gathers kindling and keeps the fire fed.

> Darkness comes before supper. Candles of wax are for the church and the lord; we have rushlights. Joan peels the rushes and dips the pith in bacon fat, and each burns about as long as the priest's Sunday sermon. Mostly, when the sun goes, we go too — to bed.

## 24. First sleep

> Our first sleep ends around midnight. We wake, check the embers, whisper, pray, sometimes tell stories in the dark. Then we sleep again until the cock crows. On the longest nights, the dark lasts sixteen hours. You cannot sleep through all of it, so you learn to share it.

> Then comes Christmas, and for twelve days the lord's work stops. I bring my hen to the manor, as my rent demands. In return, on Christmas Day, the tenants eat at the lord's hall. We bring our own bread, cup and firewood, and we eat meat until we can barely stand.

> Then, at Candlemas, in early February, the light changes. The afternoons stretch a little longer. The ice on the river groans and cracks. In store we have half a sack of oats, a little bacon, some hay. It is not much. But it is enough.

## 31. Warmth (POV)

> You might wonder how we live without heating. But we are not without warmth. We have it in our wool, our thatch, our fire that never dies, our animals, and in each other. We do not fight winter. We prepare for it all year, and then we wait it out, together.
